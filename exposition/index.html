Spectral graph theory, checked in Lean 4

A gap that was supposed to be 2 is 1

Every network has two numbers attached to it that describe its shape. A conjecture written by a computer and published on 2 November 2026 says they always stay at least 2 apart. They can come as close as 1 and no closer, and a proof checker has verified every step of that.

Sulaymon Sadullo, November 2026. Also in this project: the paper, what the theorem is good for, and the Lean proof.

In brief

  • The conjecture: for every connected graph, λ2 ≤ μ1 − 2.
  • It is false. The smallest counterexample we know has 11 vertices, and there is one of every size from 11 up.
  • The true statement: λ2 ≤ μ1 − c holds for all connected graphs exactly when c ≤ 1.
  • The proofs are written in Lean 4 and machine-checked: about 1,950 lines, no gaps.

Two numbers for every network

A graph is a set of dots, called vertices, with lines, called edges, joining some pairs of them. A road map is a graph, and so is a friendship network. All graphs here are connected: you can get from any vertex to any other by following edges.

Write a number on every vertex and call the result a pattern. Two scoring rules turn a pattern into a single number.

The agreement score. For each edge, multiply the numbers at its two ends and double the product. Add these up over all edges, then divide by the sum of the squares of all the numbers. A pattern scores well when vertices joined by an edge carry large numbers of the same sign.

The difference score. For each edge, subtract the number at one end from the number at the other and square the result. Add these up and divide by the same sum of squares. A pattern scores well when vertices joined by an edge carry very different numbers.

agreement = 2 · Σedges uv xu xvΣv xv2     difference = Σedges uv (xu − xv)2Σv xv2

Three numbers come out of these rules.

  • λ1 is the highest agreement score any pattern can reach.
  • λ2 is the runner-up: the highest agreement score among patterns perpendicular to the winner. There is a simpler test for it. Find two patterns that sit on two separate parts of the graph, with no edge running from one part to the other. If both score at least 5, then λ2 is at least 5. So λ2 is large when the graph contains two separate dense regions.
  • μ1 is the highest difference score any pattern can reach.

In the usual vocabulary, λ1 and λ2 are the two largest eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of the graph, μ1 is the largest eigenvalue of its Laplacian matrix, and the two scores are called Rayleigh quotients.

The busiest vertex controls both

Let Δ be the largest number of edges at any single vertex, the maximum degree. Both scores answer to it.

No pattern has agreement score above Δ. For any two numbers, twice their product is at most the sum of their squares, so each edge contributes at most the squares at its two ends. A vertex's square is counted once for every edge at that vertex, at most Δ times. Hence λ2 ≤ λ1 ≤ Δ.

Some pattern has difference score at least Δ + 1, as Figure 1 shows. Hence μ1 ≥ Δ + 1, a theorem of Grone and Merris from 1994.

Put the two facts together and you have the starting point of this story: for every connected graph, λ2 ≤ μ1 − 1. The two numbers are always at least 1 apart.

The conjecture, and why it looked safe

Elie Duhamel and Tristan Cazenave built a program that writes inequalities between graph quantities and then hunts for graphs that break them. Their corpus holds 305,755 such conjectures. On 2 November 2026 Cazenave posted a paper reporting that an automated pipeline had refuted 162,964 of them and proved 117,390. From the ones left standing he picked seven as the most interesting. The second reads:

Conjecture 2. For every connected graph, λ2 ≤ μ1 − 2.

That is the known inequality with a 2 where the 1 was. Call μ1 − λ2 the gap. The conjecture says the gap never drops below 2, and the evidence was good.

  • Paths. A path of 10 vertices in a row has gap 2.22. With 100 vertices it is 2.003. The gap sinks toward 2 and stays above it.
  • Barbells. Two tightly knit groups joined by a path behave the same way. The one behind Figure 2 has gap 2.04, and a longer path gives 2.0001.
  • Brute force. The author tested every connected graph with at most 9 vertices, more than a quarter of a million of them, and structured families with up to 2,000 vertices.

Two weaker versions sat in the same corpus, also open: the same inequality with √2 ≈ 1.414 and with 4√2 ≈ 1.189 in place of 2. The √2 version is printed in the table of selected conjectures in Duhamel and Cazenave's workshop paper.

Eleven vertices that break it

Start from the barbell made of two groups of four vertices, every pair inside a group joined, with a path of three vertices between the groups. Delete one edge beside each joint: the edge from the vertex where the path attaches to one of its group mates. Figure 2 shows the result, which we call G11.

For G11 a computer gives λ2 ≈ 2.6412 and μ1 ≈ 4.6180. The gap is 1.9768, and the conjecture needs 2.

Why one edge matters

In the barbell the two joint vertices have 4 edges and every other vertex has at most 3. So Δ = 4, and the busiest-vertex argument forces μ1 ≥ 5. The true value is 5.13. Each group of four keeps λ2 near 3, and the true value is 3.09. The gap comes out just above 2.

Delete the edge and the joint vertex has 3 edges like its neighbours. Now Δ = 3 and μ1 is free to fall, to 4.62. The damaged group still holds together well enough to keep λ2 at 2.64. So μ1 lost 0.51 while λ2 lost 0.44, and that difference of 0.07 carries the gap from 2.04 to 1.98.

The author's test families contained barbells, and barbells make the conjecture look sharp. Figure 3 shows their gap settling onto 2 from above. The counterexample sits one edge away from them.

A proof that does not trust decimals

Those decimals come from floating point arithmetic, which proves nothing. The proof replaces them with two certificates made of fractions.

A ceiling for μ1. No pattern on G11 has difference score above 37/8 = 4.625. The certificate is an identity: 37/8 times the sum of squares, minus the sum of squared differences, equals a sum of 17 squared expressions with positive coefficients. A sum of squares is never negative, so the difference score never exceeds 37/8.

A floor for λ2. Figure 4 shows a pattern that lives on the left group. Its agreement score is 293/111 ≈ 2.6396. The mirror image on the right group scores the same, and no edge joins the two patterns. By the two-pattern test, λ2 ≥ 293/111.

The ceiling minus the floor is 37/8 − 293/111 = 1763/888 ≈ 1.9854. The gap of G11 is therefore below 2 by at least 13/888, and Conjecture 2 is false.

Every size from 11 up

Make the path longer and nothing breaks. The ceiling 37/8 and the floor 293/111 hold for a path of any length, and the proof handles all lengths at once by induction along the path. A path with k vertices gives a graph with k + 8 vertices, and k can be anything from 3 up. So Conjecture 2 fails on n vertices for every n ≥ 11.

It is not known whether 11 is the smallest possible size. The author's exhaustive test covers 9 vertices. Our own searches at 10 vertices found nothing, and they were not exhaustive.

How low can the gap go?

The deleted-edge trick works better with bigger groups, but in our computations it levels off near 1.62. Getting to 1 takes a different design, and the busiest-vertex argument says what it must look like. For the gap to be near 1, λ2 has to sit just below Δ and μ1 just above Δ + 1.

  • λ2 just below Δ: the graph needs two separate regions where nearly every vertex has nearly Δ edges.
  • μ1 just above Δ + 1: this is fragile. Hang anything on a vertex that already has Δ edges and Δ goes up by one.

A cluster does both. Take m vertices, all joined to each other, and call them the core. Add s more vertices, the satellites, each joined to every core vertex and to no other satellite. A core vertex has m + s − 1 edges, and that is Δ. A satellite has only m, so one more edge on a satellite leaves Δ alone. Now take two clusters and one extra vertex, the connector, joined to a single satellite of each.

For this graph we prove two bounds:

μ1 ≤ m + s + 3s      λ2 ≥ m(m − 1) + 2msm + s

The right-hand side of the second bound is the average number of neighbours a vertex has inside its own cluster. Subtract and the gap satisfies

μ1 − λ2 ≤ 1 + 3s + s2 − sm + s

The term 3/s is what the two connector edges cost, diluted over s satellites. The last term is what it costs that satellites have fewer edges than core vertices. Choose s large and then m much larger, say m = s3, and both terms shrink to nothing.

TheoremThe inequality λ2 ≤ μ1 − c holds for every connected graph with at least 2 vertices if and only if c ≤ 1.

That makes 1 the best possible constant. Conjecture 2 asks for c = 2 and fails. The versions with √2 and 4√2 fail too. In our computations the smallest cluster graphs that go below each level have 13 vertices for 2, 203 for √2 and 2,139 for 4√2.

The gap never equals 1. In a connected graph λ2 is strictly smaller than λ1, and λ1 is at most Δ. The graphs above come as close as you like without arriving.

What the computer checked

The proofs are written in Lean 4. Lean is a programming language for mathematics: you state a theorem, you write its proof, and a small trusted program called the kernel checks every logical step. The library Mathlib supplies the definitions of graphs, matrices and eigenvalues. Our files add about 1,950 lines on top of it. Here are the two definitions and the main statement as they stand in the project:

noncomputable def lambda₂ {n : ℕ} (G : SimpleGraph (Fin n)) [DecidableRel G.Adj]
    (h : 2 ≤ n) : ℝ :=
  (G.isHermitian_adjMatrix ℝ).eigenvalues₀
    ⟨1, lt_of_lt_of_eq (Nat.lt_of_succ_le h) (Fintype.card_fin n).symm⟩

noncomputable def mu₁ {n : ℕ} (G : SimpleGraph (Fin n)) [DecidableRel G.Adj]
    (h : 2 ≤ n) : ℝ :=
  (G.isHermitian_lapMatrix ℝ).eigenvalues₀
    ⟨0, lt_of_lt_of_eq (Nat.lt_of_lt_of_le Nat.zero_lt_two h) (Fintype.card_fin n).symm⟩

theorem best_constant (c : ℝ) :
    (∀ (n : ℕ) (hn : 2 ≤ n) (G : SimpleGraph (Fin n)) [DecidableRel G.Adj],
        G.Connected → lambda₂ G hn ≤ mu₁ G hn - c) ↔ c ≤ 1

In Mathlib, eigenvalues₀ is the list of eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix in decreasing order, counted from 0. Position 1 of the adjacency matrix is λ2 and position 0 of the Laplacian matrix is μ1. The last line of each definition only proves that this position exists when n ≥ 2. The theorem then reads: for every real number c, the claim that every connected graph on n ≥ 2 numbered vertices satisfies λ2 ≤ μ1 − c is true if and only if c ≤ 1.

Four checks back this up. Lean lists the axioms each proof depends on, and ours depend only on the three standard ones. The proof files contain no sorry, Lean's placeholder for a missing proof. We fed Lean twelve deliberately false variants, such as the claim that the constant 1 fails as well, and it rejected all twelve. And a separate tool, Lean's comparator, confirmed that what is proved is word for word what a short file of statements claims, and replayed the proofs through two more independent checkers.

One thing Lean cannot check is whether the formal statement says what the English says. A person has to read it, and it is short enough to read.

What is new and what is not

The inequality with constant 1 was known. Grone and Merris proved μ1 ≥ Δ + 1 in 1994, and λ1 ≤ Δ is older still. Cazenave's own paper contains a stronger fact, μ2 ≥ λ2 + 1 for the second largest Laplacian eigenvalue, and remarks that it "only gives" the constant 1.

The new part, as far as we could find, is that nothing above 1 works, which settles Conjecture 2 and its two weaker forms. A literature search on 18 November 2026 turned up no earlier counterexample and no earlier statement of the sharp constant. That search took a few hours. It is not a guarantee.

A computer search over graphs found the first counterexample. The proofs, the Lean code and this page were written by an AI system, Claude, directed by the author. Every formal proof was then checked by Lean.

Loose ends

  • Does a counterexample with 10 vertices exist?
  • We ran these graphs against all 14,150 open conjectures of the corpus. In floating-point arithmetic 125 of them fail, Conjecture 2 and its two weaker forms among them. Eighteen of the others are proved by hand in the paper, and the rest are numerical findings. The details are on the companion page, what the theorem is good for.

Words used here

Graph, vertex, edge
Dots and the lines joining some pairs of them. No edge joins a vertex to itself, and two vertices share at most one edge.
Connected
Every vertex can be reached from every other by following edges.
Degree, and Δ
The degree of a vertex is its number of edges. Δ is the largest degree in the graph.
Adjacency matrix
A table with one row and one column per vertex: 1 where two vertices share an edge, 0 otherwise. Its eigenvalues, largest first, are λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ …
Laplacian matrix
The degrees on the diagonal, minus the adjacency matrix. Its largest eigenvalue is μ1.
Eigenvalue
A number attached to a matrix. For the symmetric matrices here, the largest eigenvalue is the best score any pattern can reach, and the later ones are the best scores under added restrictions.
Rayleigh quotient
The technical name for the agreement and difference scores.
Path, barbell
A path is a row of vertices, each joined to the next. A barbell is two complete groups joined by a path.
Counterexample
One case in which a claimed rule fails. One is enough to make the rule false.
Lean 4, Mathlib
A proof checker and its library of formalised mathematics.
