Potential applications

What the gap theorem is good for

The theorem says that two numbers attached to every connected graph, λ2 and μ1, are always more than 1 apart and can come as close to 1 as you like. Its most direct use is as a test. The graphs built for the proof break 125 of the open conjectures in the same computer-generated corpus, Conjecture 2 among them. This page says what follows from the result, what we measured, and where the effect is too small to matter.

A test set for machine-made conjectures

The corpus of Duhamel and Cazenave holds 305,755 conjectures. Each is an inequality between graph quantities, written in a small formula language. Its archive marks 14,150 of them as open: not proved, and not refuted on the authors' test graphs. We wrote our own evaluator for the formula language and ran every open conjecture on the graphs that came out of the proof.

  • Deleted-edge barbells. Two complete groups joined by a path, each missing one edge at the vertex where the path attaches. The 11-vertex counterexample to Conjecture 2 is one of them.
  • Cluster graphs. Two clusters, each a complete core plus satellites joined to the whole core, linked through one connector vertex. These are the graphs that show the constant 1 is best possible.
  • Clusters on a longer path. The same clusters with more than one vertex between them.

As a control we ran plain barbells: two complete graphs joined by one edge or a short path. They use none of our ideas.

The count

Of the 14,150 open conjectures, 125 fail on our graphs. They split in two.

For 22 of them the construction matters. No plain barbell we tried breaks them, at any size up to two million vertices.

The other 103 fail on plain barbells as well. They owe nothing to the theorem and fail for size alone. The corpus's own test set contains two complete graphs joined by an edge, but only up to 30 vertices a side, and every one of these 103 needs at least 62 vertices.

How we know the evaluator is right

The evaluator is ours, written from the description of the formula language. Three checks tie it to the corpus.

  • The archive records, for each refuted conjecture, the graph that refutes it and by how much. For the 61,115 whose recorded graph has at most 6 vertices, our evaluator reproduces the recorded amount in 61,074 cases. The 41 exceptions are formulas evaluated exactly at a singular point, such as the logarithm of 1 or a division by an eigenvalue that is exactly 0, where rounding decides the answer.
  • None of the 117,390 conjectures that the archive marks as proved fails on any of the 1,151 graphs in our two main runs.
  • A failure counts only if the left side exceeds the right by more than 10−6, the corpus's own threshold, and still does so after every eigenvalue and the Randić index have been nudged by one part in a billion. Among the conjectures that are not proved in Lean, the smallest excess is 0.0011.

These are floating-point results. Four of the 125 are also proved false in Lean, as part of the main theorem. Eighteen more, the rest of the 22, are proved false in the paper from exact formulas, without Lean. The other 103 are numerical findings and should be read as such.

Why the middle group falls

All 17 conjectures in the middle group say the same kind of thing: the Randić index R exceeds λ2 by more than 1 once the radius or the diameter is large enough. Cluster graphs with two satellites per cluster refuse. Their radius is 3 and their diameter is 6 whatever their size, and R − λ2 sinks toward 1: we computed 1.0142 at 20,005 vertices and 1.0014 at two million.

This touches Conjecture 1 of the source paper, R ≥ λ2 + 1. It survives on every graph we tried, by a margin that shrinks to 0.0014. The paper's author had seen the same on two cliques joined by an edge. Cluster graphs show it at radius 3, which is the case the 17 conjectures were betting against.

A counterexample with ten vertices

Conjecture 1053490 of the corpus says λ2 ≤ R − rad/2. Take two copies of the complete graph on 5 vertices, delete one edge in each, and join the copies by an edge between one end of each deleted edge. The result has 10 vertices and 19 edges.

Its Randić index is exactly 13/4 + √3 and its radius is 3, so the right side of the conjecture is 7/4 + √3 ≈ 3.4821. Its λ2 is about 3.5171, which is larger.

This one can be settled without decimals. The pattern in Figure 2 has agreement score 146244/41581. The same pattern with all signs positive scores more, the two patterns are perpendicular, and neither interacts with the other. By the two-pattern test, λ2 ≥ 146244/41581. Finally (146244/41581 − 7/4)2 = 86382500281/27663672976, which is greater than 3, so λ2 > 7/4 + √3 and the conjecture fails on this graph.

The corpus's authors tested every graph with at most 9 vertices, so 10 is the first size at which this could happen. The certificate is exact arithmetic, checked by a script. It is not formalized in Lean.

What the graphs do not break

They are not a universal weapon. The source paper highlights seven conjectures. Our graphs kill the second. The other six hold on all 1,128 graphs we tried them on, and so do the two stronger forms of Conjecture 1 that the paper proposes. None of the 3,798 conjectures that the archive proves from an unproved lemma fails either.

A tool that came out of it

Graphs like these consist of a few classes of interchangeable vertices. Their eigenvalues, Randić index, rank, radius and diameter can then be read off a small matrix with one row per class: 7 rows for a cluster graph, whatever its number of vertices. On the 206 graphs where we could compare, the shortcut agrees with the direct computation to 15 digits. With it, about 135,000 conjectures were checked against 215 graphs of up to two million vertices, thirteen times over, in a little over a minute on a laptop.

The corpus's authors could add such graphs to their test set at almost no cost. The same goes for anyone who generates conjectures of this kind: test on large graphs with few vertex classes before calling a conjecture open.

What follows inside the subject

The best straight-line bound

A two-line consequence of the Lean theorems, not itself formalized

No inequality of the form λ2 ≤ a · μ1 + b with a < 1 holds for all connected graphs. With a = 1 the best possible b is −1. The reason: on cluster graphs μ1 grows without limit while μ1 − λ2 stays below 1 + ε. So the line λ2 = μ1 − 1 is the best linear relation between the two numbers.

Theorem 1 of the source paper is tight at every scale

Follows from the Lean theorems and the paper's Theorem 1

The source paper proves μ2 ≥ λ2 + 1, where μ2 is the second largest Laplacian eigenvalue, and notes that stars give equality. Stars have λ2 = 0. Because μ2 ≤ μ1, cluster graphs show the same tightness with λ2 as large as you like. We computed μ2 − λ2 = 1.0199 for a cluster graph with two million vertices.

An exact value for λ2

Short argument, checked numerically on 351 graphs, not formalized

In a cluster graph with core size m and s satellites, λ2 equals the largest eigenvalue of one cluster on its own:

λ2 = (m − 1) + √((m − 1)2 + 4ms)2

Delete the connector and two separate clusters remain, whose two largest eigenvalues are equal. Interlacing squeezes λ2 of the whole graph between them. This is sharper than the bound the Lean proof uses, which is the average degree of a cluster.

Conjecture 1 would be tight at radius 3

Numerical

As described above: on cluster graphs with two satellites, R − λ2 tends to 1 with radius 3 and diameter 6. If Conjecture 1 is true, no extra term that depends on the radius or the diameter can be added to it.

Reusable pieces of the Lean proof

Three parts of the formalization do not depend on this problem.

  • Two eigenvalue tests. If the score of every pattern is at most c, every eigenvalue is at most c. If two perpendicular patterns that do not interact both score at least c, the second largest eigenvalue is at least c. Both are derived from Mathlib's spectral theorem in one file, Rayleigh.lean, and stated for any real symmetric matrix. A formal bound on an eigenvalue of a concrete graph can start from them.
  • Two classical lemmas the corpus still lacks in Lean. The corpus ships its own Lean library. Its lemma table lists l1_deg (2m/n ≤ λ1 ≤ Δ) and mu1_deg (Δ + 1 ≤ μ1 ≤ min(n, 2Δ)) without a Lean theorem, and counts 4,113 and 1,933 proofs that use them. Our file BestConstant.lean proves one half of each: every adjacency eigenvalue is at most Δ, and μ1 ≥ Δ + 1. The archive's notes say 37,885 of its proofs are not yet verified in Lean, in most cases because a lemma has not been formalized or connected yet, and they name l1_deg as an example. The two projects use different Mathlib versions and different definitions of the invariants, so the lemmas would need adapting.
  • A way to move certificates between vertex sets. The cluster graph is easiest to describe with structured vertex names. Transfer.lean carries a bound proved there over to the numbered vertices that the final statement uses.

The method also transfers: find a counterexample numerically, turn it into fractions, and let Lean check the fractions. For an upper bound on μ1 the certificate is a sum of squares. For a lower bound on λ2 it is a pair of vectors.

Outside mathematics

Both numbers have day jobs. Here is where they meet and what the theorem says there. Nothing in this section has been tested on real networks.

μ1 is a hard limit in several places.

  • Averaging over a network. Each node repeatedly moves its value toward its neighbours' values by a step α. The values converge to the average exactly when α is below 2/μ1, because the update multiplies each mode by 1 − αμ and the fastest mode has μ = μ1. Xiao and Boyd (2004) is the standard reference.
  • Synchronization. For a broad class of coupled oscillators, the synchronized state is stable when the ratio of μ1 to the smallest non-zero Laplacian eigenvalue is below a threshold set by the oscillator (Barahona and Pecora, 2002).
  • Filters on graphs. Chebyshev filters, used in graph neural networks, rescale the Laplacian by its largest eigenvalue before applying a polynomial (Defferrard, Bresson and Vandergheynst, 2016). That eigenvalue is μ1 when the plain Laplacian is used.
  • Cuts. No cut of a graph with n vertices has more than n · μ1/4 edges (Mohar and Poljak, 1990).

λ2 measures something else: it is large when the network has two separate dense groups.

The old inequality already tied the two together: two dense groups with λ2 force μ1 above λ2 + 1. Conjecture 2 would have licensed a stricter rule of thumb, λ2 + 2. The theorem says the floor is λ2 + 1 and no higher, and its graphs show how a network gets down to that floor: inside each group every node has about the same number of links, and the link between the groups hangs on nodes that have fewer.

A small worked case. In the 11-vertex barbell the limit on the averaging step is 2/5.128 = 0.390. Remove the one edge at each joint and the limit is 2/4.618 = 0.433, which is 11% larger, because the busiest vertex is no longer the one that carries the bridge.

The honest size of all this is one unit of μ1. That is about a fifth of μ1 when the busiest node has 3 links, and under one percent when it has 100. For large dense networks it is noise.

What we did not do

  • Only 4 of the 125 refutations are checked in Lean. Another 18 are proved by hand in the paper. The remaining 103 are floating-point findings with the margins stated above.
  • "Open" means open in the archive published with the paper of 2 November 2026. Some of these conjectures may have been settled since.
  • We did not look for the smallest possible counterexample to each conjecture, only among the graphs we tested.
  • The split into 22 and 103 depends on the plain barbells we tried: 190 of them, from 6 to two million vertices. A barbell we did not try could move a conjecture from the first group to the second.
  • 19 further open conjectures fail on plain barbells and not on our graphs. They are not counted in the 125.
  • We have not shown these results to the corpus's authors.
